Your 3 ft walkway survives everywhere

At ground: 10 ft front, 8 ft rear, 4 ft each side. At the upper floors: 4 ft on all four sides. Every face of the building keeps more than the 3 ft you need to walk round it and maintain it.

Two columns instead of a 6 ft cantilever

Hanging three storeys off a 6 ft cantilever is heavy and expensive. Two columns standing in the apron on the new front line carry it instead, and they leave 19'-3" of clear opening — far more than the gate needs. The rear 4 ft is small enough to simply cantilever, no columns.

The left column hides behind the screen

It sits directly behind the black louvre spine, so the screen still runs unbroken from ground to roof in one plane. That line is the thing that makes the building look like the reference; a 6 ft step in it at first-floor level would have killed it.

Site plan — two build lines on the real boundary

Plot B is not a rectangle. It is 34'-8" wide at the street, 37'-11" at the back, and one side is nearly 8 ft longer than the other. The building is still set out square — you never build a skewed building to match a skewed plot — and the irregularity is absorbed in the setbacks.

The survey says it fits, with room over

You need 72 ft of depth for a 4 ft front setback, 64 ft of building and 4 ft at the back. The centreline gives 78 ft. Side setbacks come out at 3'-11" at the street, opening to about 5'-0" at the back — above your 3 ft walkway on every face. The rear setback runs 5'-5" at the east corner to 11'-1" at the west, because the back boundary is skewed.

Square the building to the street, lean the boundary

The two side boundaries both lean the same way but by different amounts, which is why the plot fans out. Set the building out square and let each side setback vary — that is how it is drawn here. Trying to make the building follow the boundary would put a kink in every wall and every window.

Ask this at the planning office first

Is the setback measured to the wall, or to the furthest projection? Most assemblies measure to the projection. If yours does, the 4 ft becomes the binding number for the whole building and this scheme needs changing — either a smaller overhang, or the ground floor brought forward too. It is one question, and it decides the drawing.

If the answer goes against us

Fallback A: cut the overhang to 3 ft front and back, which most regimes allow as a projection. You keep 190 ft² per floor of the 270. Fallback B: bring the ground floor forward as well and accept 4 ft all round — you lose the open apron but the car park grows to 64 ft deep.

02

Ground floor — car park, and nothing else

Every square foot is parking bar the stair and a store. Three lanes nose-to-tail give seven covered bays plus one on the apron — two cars per flat. Why three lanes and not four

Four lanes across 29'-8" gives 7'-5" each. A car is 5'-9" to 6'-6" wide, so nobody could open a door. Three lanes at nearly 10 ft are generous instead, which is what makes the tandem bays usable — you can walk down the side of a parked car.

Read the bay lengths before you count on seven

The middle and east lanes are three deep at 15'-7" a bay. That suits a saloon (a Corolla is 14'-9") and most mid-size SUVs. A full-size Land Cruiser is 16'-4" and will not go three deep — those two lanes would take two each, giving five covered rather than seven. Bay 1 in the west lane is 19'-10" and takes anything on the road. Worth knowing what your tenants actually drive.

Cars will block each other, by design

Nose-to-tail is the only way to reach seven, so the front car in each lane owns the ones behind it. That is your call and it is the right trade for this plot — three independent bays was the alternative. What is not acceptable is a bay with no route out at all, which is what the last version had. Number the bays and give every tenant their neighbour's phone number.

The stair moved again, and this time it fixed a real error

The street-end position looked tidy and was wrong: it stood across the mouth of the west lane, so the two bays behind it had no way out at all — not blocked by a neighbour, landlocked. Moving it to the middle of that lane gives bay 1 a clear run to the gate, turns the dead 15'-10" behind it into a store, and is the one position the one-bedroom floor and the two-bedroom floors can both use. Usable covered bays go from six to seven. The cost is a 20 ft walk up the lane past one car instead of stepping straight in off the street.

The front door is now in the middle of the flat

It had to move — the stair moved — and it is better where it is. You come off the landing into a hall at the centre of the plan: bedrooms and the corridor to the left, living, dining and kitchen to the right. Nothing is more than about 25 ft from the door. The old plan put the entrance in the corner of the living room, so every trip to a bedroom crossed the room people were sitting in.

Bedrooms at the back, living at the front, 70 ft² of corridor between

The two bedrooms sit against the quiet rear boundary with their suites hard against the back wall. The corridor in front of them is 3'-6" wide and 20 ft long — not the full 29'-8". It stops at the last door it has to reach, and the 34 ft² beyond became a store. The first version ran a 4 ft corridor across the whole width to serve two doors: 119 ft² of walking space in a flat where the kitchen was 87. It has a window at its west end, so it is a lit spine rather than a dark passage.

The kitchen and dining are one room, and that fixed two faults at once

Three rooms across a 29'-8" plate means the middle one has no outside wall, so the first version had a windowless dining room — and to fit it, an 87 ft² kitchen. In a 1,528 ft² two-bedroom flat that kitchen was smaller than the one in the 769 ft² flats downstairs, which is indefensible. Merged, it is a 183 ft² eat-in kitchen with a real 6 ft window on the east wall and a 36 ft² walk-in pantry off it. Same total area, and every room a person sits in now has a window of its own. If you would rather have a separate dining room than the study, say so — that swap works, but only that one: four windowed rooms will not fit across the front.

Both suites still open off their own bedroom, through one door

Neither is reached from the corridor, and neither has a second door: you enter the dressing area and walk past a 5" partition into the bath. Both are now 8'-0" deep instead of 6'-10", which is what turns the master's into a room you can put a bath across (80 ft²) rather than a shower strip. The visitor washroom is off a short spur of the entrance hall — a guest never gets past the hall, and the spur is what buys it a real window on the east wall instead of an extract fan.

Three stacks, and the rear one goes out through the wall

The visitor washroom, the pantry, the kitchen and the laundry all sit in the east strip, directly over the one-bedroom flats' own wet zone — one stack, all four levels. The two bedroom suites are against the rear wall, and they drain out through the back of the building into an external stack boxed on the rear face. That is deliberate: it keeps a bathroom pipe out of the bedroom below it, and the rear elevation faces your own 4 ft setback and the garden, so nobody sees it.

The two flats are the same flat, mirrored

The stair core sits exactly on the middle of the depth, so one flat is a reflection of the other about that line. Every room matches its twin: living 203, bedroom 180, kitchen 153, dressing-and-bath suite 102, utility 56, lobby 48, visitor WC 27, balcony 84. Difference between the two: zero. No tenant can stand in their neighbour's flat and feel short-changed.

One wet stack for both flats

The main dashed zone runs 39'-8" down the east side: en-suite, kitchen and utility for one flat, then the same three mirrored, with the two utilities back-to-back on the mirror line. One soil pipe and one riser serve all six rooms on every floor. Moving the visitor washrooms to the west wall adds a second, much smaller stack in the corner beside the stair, carrying six WCs and nothing else. That is the price of keeping the guest WC away from the kitchen, and it is worth paying — it is one 4" pipe boxed into a corner, not a second set of runs across the plan.

Why front and back, not side by side

Splitting the plate down its length would give two units 14'-10" wide and 62'-8" deep — a 4:1 tunnel with no light in the middle. Splitting front to back gives two units roughly 30 ft square, each with windows on three sides. The stair sits between them so both front doors open off one landing.

How you move through each one

In off the landing into a proper entry lobby. The visitor washroom is immediately left, on its own, and the kitchen door is straight ahead on the right. The living room opens off the lobby through a wide cased opening and runs the west side to the balcony. The bedroom is off the living, with its own en-suite and wardrobe. A utility sits behind the kitchen. Nothing is reached through a bedroom.

The central stair is what let the two layouts share one building

This plan only works with the stair on the middle of the depth — that is what lets two units sit front and back with daylight on three sides each, and it is what makes them a true mirror. A stair must occupy the same footprint on every level, so putting one-bedroom flats on level 1 meant the whole building adopting this core: the car park's stair moved into the middle of the west lane, and the two-bedroom floors were re-planned around a mid-plan entrance. There is now one stair position and every level uses it. It cost one covered bay and it fixed two that were landlocked.

The visitor washroom is 9 ft from the kitchen

It sat beside the kitchen in the last version, which was wrong — a guest WC should not share a wall or a doorway with where food is prepared. It is now across the flat on the west wall with the entry lobby between the two. That also gets it a real window instead of a ducted extract fan, and a guest never sets foot past the lobby.

The kitchens are the point

153 ft² each, wrapping the utility as an L, with a real window on the east wall and entered off the lobby. In a one-bedroom flat the kitchen is where the tenant actually lives, and a galley off a corridor is what makes a small flat feel small.

One door, not two — dressing straight through to the bathroom

The wardrobe and the bathroom used to be separate rooms with a door each off the bedroom. They are now one suite behind a single door: you walk in through the dressing area, hanging on the left, and continue past a partition into the bath and WC. The partition is a 5" block screen that stops 3'-0" short of the far wall, so it divides the wet half from the dry half and gives the WC some privacy from the bedroom door without another leaf to open. Two fewer doors per flat, and the whole thing reads as one room instead of two cupboards.

What the utility is for, and why it changed shape

Washing machine, water heater, drying and household storage — all of it out of the kitchen and off the balcony. It was first drawn 15'-8" long by 3'-10" deep, which is 60 ft² of the right area in entirely the wrong proportion: a washing machine is 2'-0" deep, so that left 1'-10" to stand in. It is now 7'-8" × 7'-4" in the east corner — you can walk in and turn round — and it keeps the perforated-block screen, so washing dries in the shade with air moving through rather than hanging over the street.

Two things this mix changes

The parking finally works out. Six one-bedroom flats on three floors would have had seven bays between them — barely one each. Four flats and eight cars is two per flat, which on a plot this size is unusual and is worth more to a tenant than another 50 ft² of living room. It is the strongest argument for the mix.

Tell the engineer the floors are not identical. With three repeating floors, internal blockwork stacks and each wall carries straight down. Here it does not: apart from the stair core and the two long external walls, no internal wall runs continuously from level 1 to level 2. Level 2's walls land wherever they land, so its slab has to be designed to carry them rather than passing the load to walls below. That is ordinary for a framed building and it is not expensive — but it has to be said out loud before the slab is designed, because it is the one consequence of mixing layouts that a builder will not spot from the plans.

04

What actually makes it look like that

Six moves, and the overhang strengthens two of them. The undercroft is now genuinely recessed rather than merely dark, and the roof edge does the job the applied brow used to do.

01 — the blade

One third of the front is a blank wall

Full height, no openings, white textured render, 5'-6" wide. It is what makes the building read as tall and calm. Every window you add to it costs you the effect.

02 — the spine

A black louvre strip down the facade

3'-0" wide, unbroken from the first-floor slab to the roof. It screens the west end of every balcony now that the stair has moved inboard, which is what the reference does too. Below it the vehicle gate uses the same horizontal slat rhythm, so the line reads as continuous to the ground. Matte black steel flats at roughly 2½" centres.

03 — the recess

Balconies cut into the mass

Set back 6'-2", not bolted on. Clear glass balustrade at 3'-6", slim black top rail, full-height sliding doors behind. A projecting balcony gives the opposite look.

04 — the brow

The roof edge is the brow

The roof slab projects 2'-0" past the walls all round and its 16" edge is finished black with a warm LED strip underneath. It shades the top floor, which has nothing above it, and it is structure rather than an applied blade.

05 — the void

The ground floor is 6 ft back in shadow

The overhang does what render never could — the undercroft is a real recess, permanently in shade, with the mass reading as if it hovers. Seven cars sit in it, nose to tail in three lanes, with the stair and a store in the west lane and nothing else.

06 — one metal

Gate, rails, frames, roof edge: same black

The vehicle gate uses the same horizontal slat rhythm as the louvre spine above it. One matte black throughout — no bronze, no grey, no chrome. The two front columns are rendered white so they read as building, not as posts.

What the engineer needs to size

An edge beam along the new front line at every floor, spanning 19'-3" between the two columns with a 6 ft cantilever past the left one; the 6 ft slab span from the old wall line out to that beam; a 4 ft cantilever at the rear on all three floors; and two pad footings for the new columns. Consolidate the titles before you build

Owning both plots is not the same as them being one plot. If A and B stay separately registered, the side setback applies at the A / B line like any other boundary — so the 31 ft version needs the two titles amalgamated into one. Two other things ride on the same step: site coverage drops from 72% on B alone to 35% across A + B, which matters if your assembly caps it; and the west wall stops being a boundary wall, so it can keep its windows. Start it now — it is cheap at this stage and awkward once foundations are in.

09

Eight things to change from the original

The reference is designed for a dry Mexican climate and a Mexican servicing model. These are the departures worth insisting on.

Rain

The overhang changes the drainage

Roof at 1:60 minimum — 2" of fall per 10 ft. But the overhang now throws water 2 ft clear of the wall onto the apron, so the apron must fall away from the building and take a channel along the front. Outlets on the roof, a gully in every laundry, and the rear garden takes a soakaway.

Sun

The shade is now built in

Each floor shades the one below and the roof shades the top floor. If the street faces west, add the louvre screen down that side too — the overhang handles high sun, not low afternoon sun coming in sideways.

Air

Cross-ventilate every bedroom

On levels 2 and 3 both bedrooms get a side window and a door onto a corridor with its own window, so air crosses the room instead of stalling; the two rear baths take the back wall. On level 1 each flat has windows on three sides. Openable louvres top and bottom of the stair window turn the stair into a chimney.

Water

Storage the original does not have

Underground reservoir under the open part of the apron — not under the overhang, where you cannot get a tanker hose to it. Pump in the ground-floor service room, poly tanks on a roof platform sized for three days across ten washrooms, and the ground-level store behind the stair is where the pump and the pressure set live.

Power

Four meters, one riser

Separate meter per flat in a lockable ground-floor cupboard — four, not three, because level 1 has two front doors. One vertical riser beside the stair. Retrofitting this later means chasing finished walls.

Waste

Decide the drainage now

Ten washrooms on three floors is a real load, and there are three stacks to bring together, not one. Sewer connection if the street has one; otherwise size the septic tank and soakaway for ten people, and site it under the open apron where it can be desludged.

Security

Lock the undercroft

The covered apron is inviting at night, which cuts both ways. Sliding vehicle gate at the boundary, a lockable door at the foot of the stair, and light the soffit properly — the stair is now 20 ft inside the car park, so that lockable door matters more than it did.

Roof

Use the roof

Timber pergola and a barbecue up there. On a plot this size that terrace is the only outdoor space anyone gets — build it, and run the tank platform and any solar behind it at the rear.

10

Take this to your builder

In this order. The first item can invalidate the whole massing, so settle it before anything else is priced.

  • Ask whether the setback is measured to the wall or to the projection. If it is to the projection, the overhang breaks the front setback and we fall back to a 3 ft step or bring the ground floor forward. Everything else waits on this answer.
  • Confirm the setback figures themselves at the district assembly with your site plan in hand — front, rear and side.
  • Start consolidating the A and B titles. The 31 ft width, the west windows and the site-coverage figure all depend on the two plots becoming one. No neighbour's signature needed — both plots are yours — but the paperwork is on the critical path.
  • Have the surveyor peg the four corners before excavation. The boundary here is drawn from your plan of Plot B — front 34'-8", rear 37'-11", sides 72'-6" and 80'-2" — but pegs on the ground are what the builder sets out from.
  • Have the engineer design the front edge beam and the two columns before the ground-floor slab is set out — the pad footings go in at foundation stage, not later. Tell them at the same time that the internal walls do not stack between level 1 and level 2.
  • Agree the ground floor stays open. Write it into the contract. This is the item a builder will suggest infilling to "add a shop".
  • Keep the three plumbing stacks where they are. The east strip carries kitchens, laundries, pantries and the guest washrooms on all four levels; level 1 has a small second stack in the west corner; the two rear bedroom suites on levels 2 and 3 drain out through the back wall into an external stack. Moving any wet room off those lines multiplies the pipework.
  • Fall the apron away from the building and run a channel along the front — the overhang sheds its water there.
  • Fix floor-to-floor at 10'-4" for the apartments and 9'-10" at ground. Do not let it drift down to save a course of blocks.
  • Engage a licensed architect and structural engineer to turn this into stamped drawings for the permit.

What this document is, and is not

This is a design-intent drawing set: it fixes the idea, the arrangement and the dimensions so you and your builder are looking at the same building. It is not a construction drawing set and it cannot be submitted for a permit.
